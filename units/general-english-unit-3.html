Common writing errors

Small grammar choices can change how clear a sentence feels. This unit focuses on agreement, pronouns, articles, prepositions, modifiers and repetition, then gives a calm way to proofread.

01

Subject–verb agreement

A verb agrees with its subject in number and person. Singular “each” takes a singular verb: “Each student has a book.” Extra words between subject and verb can distract; find the subject first. In “The box of pencils is here,” box is the subject. Collective or indefinite subjects can follow course-specific conventions, so use the rule being tested.

Simple example

“The list of names is ready,” because list is the subject, not names.

Exam takeawayLocate the subject, ignore intervening phrases, then select the matching verb.
02

Pronouns and clear reference

A pronoun replaces a noun phrase, so its reader should know what it refers to. Pronouns also have grammatical roles: subject forms such as they and object forms such as them. Possessives show ownership. Singular “they” is common for an unknown person; the verb still agrees with the singular antecedent: “Each student has submitted their work.”

Simple example

“The teacher praised them” uses them as the object; “they praised the teacher” uses they as the subject.

Exam takeawayCheck the pronoun's reference, number and role in the sentence.
03

Articles: a, an and the

A and an introduce a nonspecific singular countable noun; choose between them by the first sound, not simply the first letter. The points to a particular item the reader can identify. Some nouns take no article in general statements. Article choice depends on meaning and whether the item is new, general or already known.

Simple example

“A university” starts with a /y/ sound; “an hour” starts with a vowel sound. “The book” means a specific book.

Exam takeawayExplain whether the noun is general, first-mentioned or specific.
04

Verb tense and preposition choices

Verb tense places an action in time and should stay consistent unless the time changes. Prepositions show relationships such as place, time or direction, but many occur in fixed phrases that must be learned through examples. In proofreading, check that tense matches the intended timeline and that the chosen preposition makes the relationship clear.

Simple example

“She studied yesterday and is revising today” changes tense because the time changes.

Exam takeawayIdentify the time intended and check the verb/preposition pattern in context.
05

Modifiers and sentence order

A modifier describes another word. It should sit close enough that the reader can tell what it describes. A misplaced modifier can accidentally attach to the wrong subject, while a dangling modifier has no clear word to describe. Put the person or thing doing the action beside the opening phrase.

Simple example

Unclear: “Walking to class, the rain soaked Maya.” Clear: “Walking to class, Maya was soaked by the rain.”

Exam takeawayAsk who performs the action in each opening phrase.
06

Redundancy, homophones and layered proofreading

Redundancy repeats meaning without adding information, as in “advance planning.” Homophones sound alike but differ in spelling and meaning, such as there, their and they're. Proofread in layers: first check the message and sentence structure, then agreement and tense, then articles, prepositions, word forms, spelling and punctuation. Spell-check alone cannot choose the correct meaning.

Simple example

“They left their bags there” uses their for ownership and there for a place.

Exam takeawayName the error, explain why it is wrong, then write the corrected sentence.
07

Subject–verb agreement with tricky subjects

A verb agrees with its subject, not with a nearby noun. Phrases between them can distract: “The list of topics is long” uses is because list is singular. Indefinite pronouns and compound subjects also affect agreement. Find the real subject first, then choose the verb form.

Simple example

“A box of old books was found,” because box is the subject.

Exam takeawayMark the subject and ignore intervening phrases before choosing the verb.
08

Pronoun antecedents and point of view

A pronoun should clearly refer to a noun called its antecedent and should agree with it in number and meaning. If a sentence contains two possible nouns, repeat the noun rather than leave the reader guessing. Keep point of view consistent unless there is a clear reason to change it.

Simple example

Unclear: “When Riya spoke to Nita, she was worried.” Name which person was worried.

Exam takeawayReplace an unclear pronoun with the exact noun it means.
09

Articles and countability

Use a before a consonant sound and an before a vowel sound; the choice follows sound, not just spelling. Use the when the reader can identify a particular item. Some nouns are countable and take a/an or plural forms; others are usually uncountable, such as information. Article errors often come from translating directly from another language.

Simple example

“An hour” begins with a vowel sound; “a university” begins with a consonant y-sound.

Exam takeawayCheck sound, specificity and whether the noun is countable.
10

Tense consistency and prepositions

Verb tense shows when an action happens. Keep the timeline consistent unless the meaning changes time, such as moving from past events to a present result. Prepositions form common patterns with verbs and adjectives, so learn them in phrases and check whether a time or place relationship is intended.

Simple example

“She has worked here since 2022” uses since for the starting point; “for four years” gives duration.

Exam takeawayState the intended time, then check the verb form and its natural preposition.
11

Modifiers and misplaced descriptions

A modifier should sit close to the word it describes. A misplaced modifier can make a sentence unintentionally funny or unclear; a dangling modifier has no clear word to modify. After writing an opening phrase, check that the next subject is the person or thing performing that action.

Simple example

Unclear: “Walking to class, the rain soaked Meera.” Better: “Walking to class, Meera was soaked by the rain.”

Exam takeawayName what the modifier describes and place it beside that word.
12

Homophones and commonly confused words

Homophones sound alike but differ in spelling or meaning; other commonly confused pairs look similar but have different grammatical roles. Use the sentence's meaning and grammar to choose among them. Proofreading by sound alone will not catch these errors, so check high-frequency pairs such as their/there/they're and its/it's.

Simple example

“Its cover is blue” shows possession; “It's blue” means “it is blue.”

Exam takeawayState what each form means and show the correct form in context.
13

Modifiers, adverbs and precise placement

Adjectives usually describe nouns; adverbs often describe verbs, adjectives or other adverbs. Place an adverb so the reader can tell which action or quality it modifies. Words such as only can change meaning depending on position, so put them next to the word they limit.

Simple example

“Only Riya submitted the form” differs from “Riya submitted only the form.”

Exam takeawayMove the modifier and explain the change in meaning.
14

A complete proofreading sequence

A reliable edit moves from meaning to mechanics. First check that each response answers the prompt and ideas are ordered. Next inspect sentence boundaries, agreement, pronouns, articles, tense and prepositions. Finally check spelling, capitalization and punctuation. Reading aloud catches rhythm problems; a separate pass for one error type catches patterns more reliably.

Simple example

First revise a confusing sentence for meaning, then check its verbs, and finally proofread spelling.

Exam takeawayGive the correction and the rule that explains it.
15

Subject–verb agreement

The finite verb agrees with its grammatical subject, not with a nearby noun inside a phrase. Singular subjects take singular forms in the present; plural subjects take plural forms. With either/or and neither/nor, agreement often follows the nearer subject in standard exam usage. Find the subject first, especially in long sentences.

Simple example

The list of topics is ready: list is the head subject; topics is inside a prepositional phrase.

Exam takeawayCross out interrupting phrases mentally, identify the subject head and then choose the verb.
16

Tense consistency and time signals

Tense locates an action in time and aspect shows whether it is complete, continuing or repeated. A paragraph may shift tense when the time frame changes, but an unexplained shift confuses the sequence. Time expressions such as since, already and every week provide clues, but meaning determines the final choice.

Simple example

She has studied since Monday and will sit the test tomorrow.

Exam takeawayMark when each action occurs and keep the tense consistent unless the timeline changes.
17

Articles and countability

A/an introduces one nonspecific singular count noun; the identifies something identifiable to reader and writer; plural and uncountable nouns can be general without an article. Article choice depends on sound, specificity and countability, not simply whether a word begins with a vowel letter.

Simple example

a university uses a because the opening sound is /y/; an hour uses an because the h is silent.

Exam takeawayAsk: is the noun countable, singular, and already identifiable?
18

Pronoun reference and agreement

A pronoun should clearly refer to a noun and agree with it in number and person. A vague this or they can point to more than one idea. Repeat the noun or replace the pronoun when the reader might guess. Modern singular they is widely used for an unknown person, but follow course style guidance.

Simple example

Unclear: When Riya spoke to Meera, she was late. Clear: Riya was late when she spoke to Meera.

Exam takeawayDraw an arrow from each pronoun to its intended antecedent.
19

Prepositions and verb patterns

Many verbs and adjectives take conventional prepositions or complements. These patterns should be learned as chunks because direct translation from another language often produces errors. A verb may require an infinitive, a gerund or a particular preposition. The best correction preserves intended meaning while using the established pattern.

Simple example

depend on, interested in, good at; avoid doing something, but decide to do something.

Exam takeawayCheck the governing verb or adjective and learn its pattern with a short example.
20

Punctuation changes meaning

Punctuation marks relationships between clauses and signals pauses or boundaries. A comma cannot usually join two complete sentences by itself; use a full stop, semicolon or comma plus a coordinating conjunction. Apostrophes mark possession or contractions, not ordinary plurals. Punctuation should make the sentence structure visible.

Simple example

Let's eat, friends! differs from Let's eat friends! because the comma changes who is being addressed.

Exam takeawayLocate each clause first, then choose punctuation that matches the grammar.

How the unit fits together

Connect the definitions, methods and applications before you start memorizing.

Proofreading is most effective when it follows a repeatable order. Start with meaning: does every sentence say what you intend? Then inspect sentence boundaries and clause structure. Next find the real subject and match the verb, rather than matching the nearest noun. Check pronouns for a clear antecedent and the correct role. Articles depend on sound and whether the reader can identify a noun. Modifiers should sit beside what they describe. Finally check tense, prepositions, homophones and redundancy. A spelling tool may accept both “there” and “their,” so reading aloud and asking what each word means is essential.

02

A pronoun should match its reference and grammatical role: “The teacher praised them,” where them receives the action. A and an depend on the following sound; the signals an item the reader can identify. “A university” begins with a consonant sound; “an hour” begins with a vowel sound. Prepositions often follow common patterns.

03

Put describing words beside what they describe

A misplaced modifier can confuse: “Walking to class, the rain soaked Maya” makes it sound as if the rain was walking. Place the modifier next to the person or thing it describes. Follow every step

Try each task yourself first, then compare your working with the explanation.

EXAMPLE 1

Edit a sentence in layers

TaskCorrect “Each of the players have left there bag near the door.”

  1. Meaning and subject: “Each” is singular, so use has, not have.
  2. Pronoun/possessive: their means belonging to them; there means a place.
  3. Noun agreement: in a formal exercise, “bag” can be singular after each.
  4. Read the corrected sentence aloud for a natural result.
Answer and reason

“Each player has left their bag near the door.” The verb agrees with each; their shows ownership.

EXAMPLE 2

Fix a misplaced modifier

TaskCorrect “Walking to the library, the rain soaked Sam.”

  1. The opening phrase “Walking to the library” describes the person who was walking.
  2. In the original, the grammatical subject is the rain, which makes it seem that the rain walked.
  3. Move Sam beside the modifier or rewrite the sentence.
  4. Check that the subject performs the action in the opening phrase.
Answer and reason

“Walking to the library, Sam was soaked by the rain.”

QUESTION 1Correct and explain: Each of the students have submitted their assignment.+

Correct: Each of the students has submitted an assignment. The subject is each, which is singular, so the verb is has. In formal agreement exercises, an assignment is the clearest singular noun phrase. In ordinary modern usage, singular they is also common when referring to a person whose gender is unknown, but it does not change the verb: Each student has submitted their assignment.

QUESTION 2Correct: She advised them to keep doing there best in future.+

Correct: She advised them to keep doing their best in the future. Their is a possessive determiner meaning belonging to them; there refers to a place. The phrase in the future is the usual form. Proofreading should check homophones as well as grammar, because spell-check may accept both there and their.

WORK IT THROUGH

Correct: “Each of the students have submitted there assignment.”

  1. Find the subject: each is singular, so use has.
  2. Choose the possessive their for an assignment belonging to the student.
  3. A clear revision is: “Each student has submitted their assignment.” In formal agreement exercises, “an assignment” is also clear.

WATCH OUT FOR

Easy mix-ups

  • The noun nearest the verb is not always the subject.
  • There refers to a place; their shows belonging; they're means they are.
  • Spell-check may accept the wrong homophone, so read for meaning.
