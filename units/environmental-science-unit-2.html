🌍 ENVIRONMENTAL SCIENCE & SUSTAINABILITY · UNIT II

Ecosystems & biodiversity

An ecosystem is a living neighborhood. Plants, animals, microorganisms, water, soil and sunlight interact; energy moves through food relationships while nutrients are reused. This unit connects those patterns with biodiversity and conservation.

01

Biotic and abiotic parts

An ecosystem includes biotic parts (living organisms) and abiotic conditions (light, water, air, soil, temperature and nutrients). These factors affect which organisms can survive and how they interact. A pond, forest or grassland is an ecosystem when its organisms and physical surroundings are studied together as a functioning system.

Simple example

In a pond, fish and algae are biotic; dissolved oxygen, sunlight and water temperature are abiotic.

Exam takeawayGive examples from both groups and show how they influence one another.
02

Producers, consumers and decomposers

Producers such as plants and algae capture energy, usually from sunlight, and build organic food. Consumers get energy by eating producers or other consumers. Decomposers, including many fungi and bacteria, break down dead organisms and waste, returning nutrients to the environment. These roles connect food production, feeding and nutrient reuse.

Simple example

Grass is a producer, a grasshopper is a primary consumer, a frog can be a secondary consumer and fungi decompose remains.

Exam takeawayName each role and explain its function in an ecosystem.
03

Trophic levels and energy flow

A trophic level is a feeding position, such as producer, primary consumer or predator. Energy moves from one level to another through feeding, but organisms use much of it for life processes and release heat. Less usable energy is available at higher levels, which limits how many levels a food chain can support. Energy flows through the system rather than cycling back.

Simple example

A hawk receives only part of the energy stored in the animals it eats.

Exam takeawayDraw arrows from food to eater and explain why energy decreases up the chain.
04

Food chains, food webs and ecological pyramids

A food chain is one path showing who eats whom. A food web links many chains because organisms often have several food sources and predators. Pyramids summarize the number of organisms, their biomass or available energy at each trophic level. Energy pyramids are upright because usable energy decreases at each transfer; other pyramid shapes can vary by ecosystem and measure.

Simple example

Grass → insect → frog is one chain; adding birds and snakes creates connected paths in a web.

Exam takeawayDistinguish chain from web and identify what each pyramid measures.
05

Biodiversity and ecosystem services

Biodiversity includes variety within species, among species and across ecosystems. It supports processes such as pollination, soil formation, water regulation and nutrient cycling, which also support human livelihoods. Ecosystem services describe benefits people receive from functioning ecosystems. Losing species or habitats can weaken these processes and make communities less resilient.

Simple example

Pollinating insects support reproduction in many flowering plants and crops.

Exam takeawayExplain biodiversity at more than one level and connect it to an ecosystem service.
06

Threats and conservation methods

Major threats in the guide include habitat loss, overuse, pollution, invasive species and climate change. In-situ conservation protects organisms in their natural habitats through approaches such as protected areas. Ex-situ conservation protects them outside those habitats, such as in seed banks, botanical gardens or managed breeding programs. In-situ protects ecological relationships; ex-situ can support recovery when a wild population is in severe danger.

Simple example

Protecting a wetland is in-situ; storing seeds from its plants is ex-situ.

Exam takeawayCompare where protection happens, what it preserves and how the methods can work together.
07

Ecological succession

Succession is the gradual change in the species and structure of an ecosystem over time. Primary succession begins where soil is absent, while secondary succession follows disturbance where soil remains. Early colonizers change conditions and may make it easier for later species to establish. Real ecosystems can follow different pathways depending on climate, disturbance and local species.

Simple example

After a fire, grasses and shrubs may return before mature trees, because soil and some seeds remain.

Exam takeawayDistinguish primary and secondary succession by the starting conditions.
08

Nutrient cycles and decomposers

Unlike energy, matter is reused. Decomposers such as fungi and bacteria break down dead organisms and waste, releasing nutrients to soil or water. Plants absorb nutrients, animals obtain them through food, and decomposition returns them to the environment. Human activities such as fertilizer overuse can alter these cycles and move nutrients into rivers and lakes.

Simple example

Excess fertilizer washes from a field into a pond, where nutrient enrichment can trigger algal growth.

Exam takeawayDraw a loop showing how a named nutrient moves through organisms and the environment.
09

Invasive species and keystone roles

An invasive species is introduced beyond its native range and spreads in ways that can disrupt local ecosystems. It may compete, prey on native species or change habitat. A keystone species has an effect on ecosystem structure that is unusually large relative to its abundance. These terms describe different ideas: one is about introduction and spread, the other about ecological influence.

Simple example

If a predator controls a dominant grazer, removing that predator may allow grazing pressure to reshape vegetation.

Exam takeawayDefine the term carefully and describe the ecological link, not only the species name.
10

Protected areas and habitat corridors

Protected areas conserve habitats by limiting damaging activities, but boundaries alone may not address every threat. Corridors connect separated habitat patches and can allow movement, feeding and breeding. Conservation planning also considers local communities, land rights and long-term monitoring. A useful answer explains which threat an approach addresses and what conditions make it effective.

Simple example

A wildlife corridor across a road can reconnect two forest patches and reduce isolation of populations.

Exam takeawayMatch each conservation method to the problem it is designed to reduce.
11

Biomes and ecosystem conditions

A biome is a broad ecological region shaped by climate, soils and characteristic plant and animal communities. Temperature and rainfall patterns influence which organisms can survive and how ecosystems function. Biomes are not simply lists of species: explain the environmental conditions and adaptations that link the community to its place.

Simple example

A desert community includes organisms adapted to limited water and large temperature changes.

Exam takeawayDescribe climate first, then connect adaptations and ecosystem structure.
12

Food-chain arrows and trophic roles

A food-chain arrow points from the organism being eaten toward the organism receiving energy. Producers form the base of most food chains; primary consumers eat producers, while higher consumers feed on other consumers. An organism may occupy different roles in different chains, which is why a food web gives a fuller picture.

Simple example

Grass → grasshopper → frog means energy moves from grass to grasshopper to frog.

Exam takeawayLabel trophic roles and draw arrows in the direction energy moves.
13

Biodiversity at genetic, species and ecosystem levels

Biodiversity includes variation within a species, variety among species and diversity among ecosystems. Genetic variation can help populations respond to changing conditions; species diversity supports ecological relationships; ecosystem diversity provides different habitats and services. Conservation at only one level may miss risks at another.

Simple example

Protecting a wetland preserves habitat diversity while also supporting many species and their genetic populations.

Exam takeawayName all three levels and apply each to the example in the question.
14

Ecosystem services and human wellbeing

Ecosystem services are benefits people obtain from natural systems. Provisioning services include food and materials; regulating services include flood control and climate regulation; cultural services include recreation and cultural meaning; supporting processes include soil formation and nutrient cycling. These categories help explain why an ecosystem has value beyond the products sold from it.

Simple example

A mangrove can provide fish habitat, reduce some wave impacts and support cultural or livelihood practices.

Exam takeawayName the service category and explain the process that creates the benefit.
15

Ecological pyramids of number and biomass

A pyramid of numbers counts organisms at each trophic level; it can be inverted when one large producer supports many consumers. A biomass pyramid compares the mass of living material, and it can also be inverted in some aquatic systems where small producers reproduce quickly. An energy pyramid represents energy transfer and is upright because energy is lost at each level.

Simple example

One tree can support many insects, creating an inverted pyramid of numbers at the base.

Exam takeawayState what the pyramid measures before interpreting its shape.
16

Carbon cycle and human emissions

Carbon moves among atmosphere, organisms, oceans and rocks through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, exchange and long-term geological processes. Burning fossil fuels transfers stored carbon to the atmosphere rapidly; deforestation can reduce uptake and release stored carbon. Explain the direction of transfer and distinguish short-term biological cycling from long-term storage.

Simple example

Combustion moves carbon from fossil fuel stores into atmospheric carbon dioxide.

Exam takeawayDraw reservoirs and arrows, then mark where human activity changes the rate.
17

Nitrogen cycle and soil fertility

Most organisms cannot use atmospheric nitrogen gas directly. Nitrogen fixation makes it available in biologically useful forms; plants assimilate it, animals receive it through food, and decomposition returns nitrogen compounds. Bacteria also transform nitrogen through nitrification and denitrification. Fertilizer can increase production but excess nitrogen may leach or run off.

Simple example

Nitrate from a fertilized field can enter a stream and contribute to eutrophication.

Exam takeawayName two transformations and connect excess input to an ecosystem effect.
18

Ecological succession after disturbance

Secondary succession can follow fire, storms or abandoned farmland because soil remains, while primary succession starts on a surface with little or no developed soil. Species composition changes as plants and organisms modify habitat and resources. Succession is not always a simple march toward one final community; repeated disturbances and local conditions alter the path.

Simple example

After farmland is abandoned, grasses may establish before shrubs and later woodland species.

Exam takeawayIdentify the starting condition and avoid claiming every ecosystem follows one fixed sequence.
19

Endemic, native and invasive species

A native species occurs naturally in a region; an endemic species is native to and restricted to a particular region. An invasive species is introduced and spreads in ways that cause ecological, economic or social harm. These labels are not interchangeable. Conservation priorities depend on ecological role, threat and the history of introduction.

Simple example

A native species can become invasive after human movement into a new ecosystem, depending on impacts and context.

Exam takeawayDefine the geographic relationship and the harm criterion precisely.
20

Conservation planning and community participation

Conservation is more durable when it protects habitat, monitors species and includes the people who depend on the landscape. Protected areas, corridors, restoration, seed banks and captive breeding address different problems. Plans should state the target, threat, action, responsible groups and monitoring measure, then adapt if results are poor.

Simple example

A corridor project can pair habitat restoration with agreements for safe movement across farmland.

Exam takeawayMatch the conservation method to a specific threat and evaluation measure.

How the unit fits together

Connect the definitions, methods and applications before you start memorizing.

An ecosystem is not just a list of organisms; it is a set of relationships among living things and physical conditions. Producers bring energy into most food webs, consumers transfer some of that energy, and decomposers return nutrients from dead matter. Energy transfer is inefficient because organisms use energy for movement, maintenance and heat. Nutrients such as carbon and nitrogen follow cycles through air, soil, water and organisms. Biodiversity can make these systems more resilient because different species perform different roles, though resilience does not mean ecosystems cannot be damaged. Conservation works at more than one scale: protecting habitat preserves relationships, while seed banks or breeding programs can support species when wild populations are threatened.

Quick recap: teach these links back

01

Living and non-living parts form one system

Abiotic factors include light, water, soil and temperature. Producers such as plants capture energy and build food. Consumers get energy by eating organisms. Decomposers break down dead material and return nutrients. These roles connect organisms to the surroundings they depend on.

02

Energy flows; matter cycles

A food chain shows one feeding path; a food web joins many paths. At each trophic level, some energy is used and lost as heat, so less is available higher up. Matter such as carbon and nutrients cycles through organisms and the environment. Ecological pyramids summarize number, biomass or energy; an energy pyramid is upright because usable energy decreases upward.

03

Biodiversity supports life and livelihoods

Biodiversity includes variety within species, between species and among ecosystems. Habitat loss, overuse, pollution, invasive species and climate change can reduce it. In-situ conservation protects species in their ecosystems, such as protected areas. Ex-situ conservation supports them outside that habitat, such as in seed banks or carefully managed captive-breeding programs; the approaches can complement each other.

EXAMPLE 1

Read an energy pyramid

TaskIf producers store 10,000 energy units and only about one tenth is available to the next level, estimate energy at two higher levels.

  1. Producers: 10,000 units.
  2. Primary consumers receive about 1,000 units under this simplified illustration.
  3. Secondary consumers receive about 100 units; the rest of energy at each level is used or lost as heat.
Answer and reason

The numbers illustrate decreasing available energy, not a universal exact percentage for every ecosystem.

EXAMPLE 2

Choose a conservation approach

TaskA plant population is declining because its habitat is being cleared. Propose complementary actions.

  1. Use in-situ protection to reduce habitat loss and maintain pollinators, soil and other ecological relationships.
  2. Collect seeds for an ex-situ seed bank to preserve genetic material.
  3. Restore habitat and monitor the wild population; return stored or propagated plants only when conditions support survival.
Answer and reason

Habitat protection and seed storage address different risks and can reinforce one another.

QUESTION 1Describe the structure and function of an ecosystem.+

The structure includes abiotic factors such as light, water, soil and temperature, and biotic groups such as producers, consumers and decomposers. Producers convert solar energy into chemical energy. Consumers transfer energy by feeding, while decomposers break down dead matter and help return nutrients. Food chains and food webs show feeding links. Ecosystems also provide services such as water regulation, pollination and soil formation.

QUESTION 2Compare in situ and ex situ biodiversity conservation.+

In situ conservation protects species within their natural ecosystems, for example through national parks, wildlife sanctuaries or biosphere reserves. It can conserve habitats and ecological relationships. Ex situ conservation protects species outside their natural habitats, for example in botanical gardens, seed banks or captive breeding programs. It can support recovery when wild populations are critically threatened. The methods can complement one another.

WORK IT THROUGH

Trace a simple food chain: Sun → grass → grasshopper → frog → hawk.

  1. Grass captures some sunlight and stores energy as food.
  2. The grasshopper receives some energy by eating grass; the frog receives some by eating the grasshopper.
  3. At each transfer, much energy is used or lost as heat. Decomposers return nutrients, but energy does not cycle in the same way.

WATCH OUT FOR

Easy mix-ups

  • Energy flows through an ecosystem; nutrients cycle.
  • A food web is many connected feeding paths, not one chain.
  • In-situ protects a habitat; ex-situ protects organisms away from their habitat.
