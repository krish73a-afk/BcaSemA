🗣️ GENERAL ENGLISH - I · UNIT II

Basic writing skills

Good writing helps a reader follow one idea at a time. This unit builds the sentence and paragraph tools behind clear writing: phrases and clauses, sentence types, paragraph shape, transitions, precise wording and punctuation.

PICTURE IT

A paragraph is like a sandwich: one main idea, helpful details, and a neat ending.

A paragraph is a short path with a sign at the start, useful stops along the way, and a clear ending. Each sentence should help the reader move along it.

01

Phrases, clauses and sentence fragments

A phrase is a group of related words without a complete subject–verb idea. A clause contains a subject and a verb. An independent clause expresses a complete thought; a dependent clause needs another clause. A fragment may be only a phrase or dependent clause presented as a full sentence, so check who or what acts and what happens.

Simple example

“Because the bus was late” is dependent; “Because the bus was late, Mira walked” is complete.

Exam takeawayUnderline subject and verb, then decide whether the idea can stand alone.
02

Simple, compound and complex sentences

A simple sentence has one independent clause. A compound sentence joins two independent clauses, often with a coordinator such as and, but or so. A complex sentence pairs an independent clause with at least one dependent clause. Sentence variety can show relationships between ideas, but avoid joining unrelated thoughts into a run-on sentence.

Simple example

Simple: “We revised.” Compound: “We revised, and we solved a paper.” Complex: “We revised because the exam was near.”

Exam takeawayIdentify the clauses and punctuation or conjunction that joins them.
03

The shape of a clear paragraph

A paragraph usually develops one main idea. A topic sentence states the focus; supporting sentences explain it with reasons, facts or examples; a closing or linking sentence completes the point or leads forward. Keep details that support the focus and move unrelated ideas elsewhere. A paragraph's length depends on the task, but its focus should remain clear.

Simple example

Topic: exercise supports health. Support: it strengthens the heart and can reduce tension. Close by connecting both effects.

Exam takeawayMark the topic sentence, support and closing sentence in your answer.
04

Coherence and logical order

Coherence means readers can follow how one sentence leads to the next. Arrange ideas by time, importance, comparison or cause and effect. Repeat important terms when a pronoun could be unclear, and use transitions to show the relationship. Remove a sentence that does not support the paragraph's central idea.

Simple example

First explain a problem, next its cause, then the effect and finally a solution.

Exam takeawayState the organizing order and use transitions that match the relationship.
05

Precise wording and punctuation

Precise verbs and nouns say more with fewer words. Remove fillers that do not add meaning, but keep enough explanation for the reader. Punctuation marks sentence boundaries and relationships: a comma cannot usually join two complete sentences by itself. Read aloud to notice where a sentence runs too long or stops before its thought is complete.

Simple example

Replace “made a decision to improve” with “decided to improve” when the meaning stays the same.

Exam takeawayChoose clear words, complete sentences and punctuation that makes clause boundaries visible.
06

Subject, verb and complete thought

A complete independent clause has a subject and a verb and expresses a thought that can stand alone. The subject tells who or what the clause is about; the verb states an action or condition. Long sentences can hide the subject, so locate the main verb and ask who or what performs it before checking punctuation.

Simple example

In “The students in the lab are preparing a report,” students is the subject and are preparing is the verb.

Exam takeawayUnderline the subject and main verb before repairing a fragment.
07

Run-ons, comma splices and sentence boundaries

A run-on joins independent clauses without correct punctuation. A comma splice joins them using only a comma. Repair either by making two sentences, using a semicolon when ideas are closely related, or adding a suitable conjunction. Punctuation should show how ideas relate rather than simply where a speaker pauses.

Simple example

Incorrect: “The file was missing, we searched again.” Correct: “The file was missing, so we searched again.”

Exam takeawayFind both independent clauses and choose a boundary that fits their relationship.
08

Transitions that show relationships

Transition words signal how ideas connect. “Because” introduces a reason, “therefore” signals a result, “however” signals contrast and “for example” introduces evidence. A transition cannot fix an illogical order by itself; first decide the relationship, then choose a word that accurately names it.

Simple example

“The first draft was short; however, the revised draft included evidence.”

Exam takeawayName the relationship between sentences before selecting a transition.
09

Proofreading one layer at a time

Editing is easier when you check one kind of issue at a time. First confirm meaning and paragraph order, then sentence completeness, agreement and tense, and finally spelling and punctuation. Reading aloud can reveal missing words or awkward rhythm, while reading backward word by word helps isolate spelling errors.

Simple example

On a second pass, check every verb against its subject instead of trying to fix everything at once.

Exam takeawayDescribe a specific revision and explain how it improves clarity or correctness.
10

Sentence types by clause structure

Sentence type depends on clauses, not sentence length. A simple sentence has one independent clause; a compound sentence has at least two independent clauses; a complex sentence combines an independent clause with a dependent clause; a compound-complex sentence has both relationships. Label clauses before classifying a long sentence.

Simple example

“I read, and she wrote because the test was near” is compound-complex.

Exam takeawayUnderline each finite verb, identify its clause and classify the structure.
11

Parallel structure in lists and comparisons

Parallel structure uses the same grammatical form for ideas that play the same role. It makes lists easier to read and comparisons fair. A sentence that mixes a noun, a verb phrase and an -ing phrase can sound uneven. Check each item after words such as and, or, both, either and not only.

Simple example

Parallel: “The course teaches reading, writing and speaking,” all gerunds.

Exam takeawayMake the compared elements match in grammatical form.
12

Topic sentences and paragraph evidence

A topic sentence signals the paragraph's main point and controls which details belong. Evidence may include facts, examples, explanation or a quotation, but it needs interpretation that links back to the point. A paragraph with evidence but no explanation can leave the reader unsure why the detail matters.

Simple example

Claim: trees reduce heat. Evidence: shaded streets are cooler. Explanation: shade limits direct solar heating of surfaces.

Exam takeawayUse point → evidence → explanation, and keep all three on one main idea.
13

Comma, semicolon and colon choices

A comma separates items, introductory material or clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction. A semicolon can link closely related independent clauses. A colon can introduce an explanation or list after a complete clause. Punctuation does not replace sentence structure: check whether the words before a mark form a complete clause.

Simple example

“The test was challenging; the worked examples helped.” Both sides are complete clauses.

Exam takeawayIdentify the clause boundary and explain why the chosen mark fits.
14

Paragraph unity and controlling idea

A paragraph develops one controlling idea. The topic sentence names the point; supporting sentences explain, compare or provide evidence; the closing sentence shows why the point matters or leads onward. If a sentence supports a different idea, move it to another paragraph. Unity keeps readers from having to guess the purpose.

Simple example

Topic: daily revision improves recall. Support with short spaced sessions, a course example and a result; omit unrelated advice about buying stationery.

Exam takeawayState the paragraph's main point in one sentence, then test every sentence against it.
15

Cohesion with reference and transitions

Cohesion makes sentences connect on the page. Pronouns and reference words point back to known ideas; transitions signal relationships such as addition, contrast, cause or sequence. Use the transition that matches the logic, and repeat a key noun when a pronoun could have two possible referents.

Simple example

The first method is quick. However, it uses more memory. This trade-off matters on a small device.

Exam takeawayName the relationship between ideas before choosing a connector.
16

Planning, drafting and revising

Writing improves through stages: understand the task, collect and group ideas, draft for meaning, revise structure and evidence, then edit sentence-level accuracy. Revision asks whether the answer meets its purpose; proofreading asks whether its grammar and spelling are correct. Editing every sentence while still planning can interrupt the argument.

Simple example

For a 200-word response, list three points first, give one example for each, then draft and check the word limit.

Exam takeawayShow a clear beginning, ordered support and a final check against the prompt.
17

Formal letter and email structure

A formal message makes its purpose easy to find. Use a meaningful subject line, an appropriate salutation, a short opening request or context, organized details, a clear next step and a courteous close. Keep tone respectful and avoid chat abbreviations. Include only information the recipient needs to act.

Simple example

Subject: Request for assignment extension. State course and deadline, explain briefly, suggest a new date and thank the reader.

Exam takeawayCheck audience, purpose, subject, action requested and polite sign-off.
18

Summarising without copying

A summary preserves the central claim and necessary supporting points while removing examples, repetition and minor detail. Read the whole passage first, note its structure, then restate ideas in your own words. Changing only a few words from the original is not paraphrasing and can distort meaning or create plagiarism.

Simple example

A long passage listing three recycling benefits can become: Recycling conserves materials, reduces waste and may lower energy use.

Exam takeawayRetain the author's key message, use fresh wording and avoid adding your opinion.
19

Audience, purpose and register

Audience and purpose guide vocabulary, detail, tone and format. A note to a friend can use contractions; a request to an administrator usually needs a more formal register. Register is not about making every sentence complicated. It is about matching language to the relationship and situation while remaining clear.

Simple example

Informal: Can you send the notes? Formal: Could you please share the lecture notes when convenient?

Exam takeawayIdentify who will read the text and what you want them to do or understand.

How the unit fits together

Connect the definitions, methods and applications before you start memorizing.

Clear writing is a chain of complete thoughts. A clause has a subject and verb; an independent clause can stand by itself, while a dependent clause relies on another clause. Sentence types let you show whether ideas are equal, subordinate or connected by cause. Paragraphs then group sentences around a single focus. Coherence comes from both the order of ideas and the signals between them. Transitions should name a real relationship—contrast, result, example or sequence—not decorate a sentence. Precision shortens the route from writer to reader: use a specific verb, remove empty phrases and punctuate clause boundaries so the reader can see where each thought begins and ends.

Quick recap: teach these links back

01

Sentences need complete thoughts

A phrase is a group of words without a complete subject-and-verb idea. A clause has a subject and a verb. An independent clause can stand alone; a dependent clause needs another idea. “The student revised” is complete; “because the exam was near” leaves the reader waiting.

02

Vary sentence structure without losing clarity

A simple sentence has one independent clause. A compound sentence joins independent clauses, often with a coordinating conjunction. A complex sentence combines an independent clause with a dependent clause. Variety makes writing less repetitive, but a sentence should not become so long that its main point is lost.

03

A paragraph develops one main idea

Start with a topic sentence that tells the focus. Add reasons, examples or facts. Put details in a sensible order: time, importance, or cause and effect. Transitions such as however, therefore and for example signal how ideas connect. Use punctuation to show boundaries, and remove sentences that do not help the paragraph's point.

Follow every step

Try each task yourself first, then compare your working with the explanation.

EXAMPLE 1

Repair a sentence fragment

TaskRevise “Although the experiment was repeated.”

  1. Find the signal word although; it introduces a dependent clause.
  2. Ask what happened despite the repetition—the sentence has not supplied the main idea.
  3. Add an independent clause: “Although the experiment was repeated, the result stayed the same.”
  4. Check that the comma separates the opening dependent clause from the main clause.
Answer and reason

The revision expresses a complete thought and keeps the contrast signaled by although.

EXAMPLE 2

Build a coherent paragraph

TaskWrite a short paragraph explaining why a study plan helps.

  1. Topic sentence: “A study plan makes revision easier to manage.”
  2. Give a reason: it divides a large syllabus into smaller sessions.
  3. Give an example: revise one topic, practise recall, then review mistakes.
  4. Close by connecting the method to consistency and progress.
Answer and reason

Every sentence supports one main idea; transitions show the order of reasoning.

QUESTION 1How can coherence be improved in a paragraph?+

Begin with one clear main idea. Arrange supporting points in a logical order, such as time, importance or cause and effect. Use pronouns only when their reference is clear, repeat essential terms when needed, and use transitions to show relationships. Remove sentences that do not support the main point. Read the paragraph aloud to find abrupt changes.

QUESTION 2What are simple, compound and complex sentences?+

A simple sentence has one independent clause: The student revised. A compound sentence joins independent clauses: The student revised, and she solved a paper. A complex sentence has an independent clause and a dependent clause: The student revised because the exam was near. Sentence variety can make writing clearer, but overly long sentences should be divided.

WORK IT THROUGH

Improve this rough paragraph: “Exercise is good. It helps the body. People should do it. It also helps mood.”

  1. Choose a topic sentence: “Regular exercise supports both physical and emotional health.”
  2. Add connected support: “It strengthens the heart and muscles. It can also improve mood because movement reduces tension.”
  3. Use a final sentence to close the point, then check that each sentence supports the same idea.

WATCH OUT FOR

Easy mix-ups

  • A dependent clause alone is usually a fragment.
  • Transitions show relationships; they cannot repair an unclear idea.
  • A paragraph can have several sentences but should still have one main focus.
