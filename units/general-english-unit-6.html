🗣️ GENERAL ENGLISH - I · UNIT VI

Oral communication

Speaking well is more than saying words correctly. Listeners also hear stress, rhythm and intonation, and they notice whether a speaker listens. This unit covers clear conversation and a simple structure for a presentation.

PICTURE IT

Good speaking is not only loud words; it is kind listening and clear signals.

Words are the message; your voice is the road they travel on. Clear pacing and a kind listener help the message reach the other person.

BY THE END, YOU CAN

  • Explain pronunciation, stress, rhythm and intonation.
  • Practise attentive listening and respectful turn-taking.
  • Plan a presentation with a purpose, signposts, examples and a closing.

01

Pronunciation and articulation

Pronunciation concerns how speech sounds and words are produced. Clear articulation helps listeners distinguish sounds and words. Accent differences are normal; the communication goal is understandable speech suited to the listener and setting. Practising difficult words slowly, then in a sentence, helps connect sound to natural speech.

Simple example

Say a new technical word slowly, break it into syllables, then use it in a full sentence.

Exam takeawayExplain pronunciation as sound production and connect it to intelligibility.
02

Word stress and sentence stress

Stress gives a syllable or word extra prominence. In a word, changing which syllable is stressed can change how it sounds and sometimes its word class. In a sentence, stress can show which information is new or contrasted. Stress is not simply shouting; it combines emphasis, duration and pitch.

Simple example

“I wanted the BLUE notebook” stresses blue to contrast its color with another one.

Exam takeawayDefine stress and explain how it can highlight a syllable or key word.
03

Rhythm and intonation

Rhythm is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in connected speech. Intonation is the movement of pitch across a phrase or sentence. Together they help listeners hear focus, contrast, a question, a conclusion or uncertainty. Effective speech uses these features naturally and clearly rather than keeping every word equally strong.

Simple example

A rising pitch at the end can suggest a question; a falling pitch may mark a finished statement.

Exam takeawayKeep rhythm (stress pattern) distinct from intonation (pitch movement).
04

Listening and conversation skills

Good conversation includes listening fully, noticing the speaker's point and asking relevant follow-up questions. Let others finish before responding. If a message is unclear, ask for clarification rather than guessing. Respectful turn-taking helps people exchange ideas and prevents one voice from taking over.

Simple example

“You mentioned the survey changed the result. What changed in the questions?” is a focused follow-up.

Exam takeawayDescribe attentive listening, relevant questions and avoiding interruption.
05

Workplace communication and presentations

Workplace communication should be respectful, concise and suited to the listener. A formal presentation has a purpose and planned structure: introduce the topic, signpost sections, explain a few points with examples and finish with a summary or action. A casual conversation is more flexible, but both require clear speech and attention to the audience.

Simple example

A presentation can say “First, the problem; next, the evidence; finally, our recommendation” to guide listeners.

Exam takeawayCompare planned structure and audience adaptation with informal conversation.
06

Articulation and clear pronunciation

Articulation is how speech sounds are formed by the tongue, lips, teeth, palate and airflow. Clear speech does not require copying another accent; it requires producing sounds clearly enough for the listener to understand. Practice difficult sound contrasts slowly, then place them in words and sentences at a natural pace.

Simple example

Say “ship” and “sip” slowly, notice the vowel difference, then practise both in a short sentence.

Exam takeawayExplain pronunciation as clear sound production and give a practice method.
07

Word stress and sentence focus

Word stress gives one syllable greater prominence, while sentence stress highlights the words that carry the important message. A change in stress can change which part of a sentence the speaker corrects or emphasizes. Use a dictionary or listening model for unfamiliar word stress and avoid stressing every word equally.

Simple example

“I asked for the BLUE file” emphasizes the file color; “I asked for the blue FILE” may contrast it with another object.

Exam takeawayMark the stressed syllable or word and explain how emphasis affects meaning.
08

Intonation and conversational meaning

Intonation is the rise and fall of pitch across a phrase. It can signal a question, completion, uncertainty, politeness or contrast, depending on language and context. The same words can sound like a genuine question or a sarcastic comment when pitch changes. Practise by listening to a speaker and marking where pitch rises or falls.

Simple example

A rising pitch at the end can make “You finished?” sound like a question.

Exam takeawayDescribe the pitch movement and its likely communicative effect.
09

Active listening and turn-taking

Good conversation involves listening for the speaker's main point, giving relevant responses and allowing balanced turns. Clarifying questions check understanding; paraphrasing shows what you heard. Avoid interrupting or planning a reply so early that you miss the speaker's meaning. In a group, invite quieter participants and keep comments connected to the discussion.

Simple example

“So your main concern is timing; have I understood you correctly?” checks understanding before replying.

Exam takeawayName a listening behavior and explain how it supports communication.
10

Vowels, consonants and articulation

Speech sounds can be described by how the vocal tract produces them. Vowels use relatively open airflow and differ by tongue height, position and lip shape; consonants involve a constriction or closure. Learning the physical contrast can make pronunciation practice more precise than repeating a word without noticing what changed.

Simple example

For /p/ and /b/, the lips close in the same place, but voicing differs.

Exam takeawayName the articulation feature and use a minimal pair to demonstrate it.
11

Syllables and word-stress patterns

A syllable is a spoken beat organized around a vowel sound. In longer words, one syllable often receives stronger stress through loudness, length or pitch. Stress patterns can distinguish words or make speech easier to follow. Use a trusted pronunciation model because stress is not always predictable from spelling.

Simple example

“REcord” as a noun and “reCORD” as a verb differ in stress in common pronunciation.

Exam takeawayMark the stressed syllable and state how stress changes the word or rhythm.
12

Pauses, rhythm and phrasing

Pauses group words into meaningful units and give listeners time to process information. English rhythm often gives stronger prominence to content words while reducing some function words, though natural speech varies. Pausing at clause boundaries improves clarity; pausing in the middle of a name or phrase can confuse the listener.

Simple example

Pause after “When the results arrived,” before stating what happened.

Exam takeawayMark phrase groups and explain how the pause supports meaning.
13

Presentation opening, structure and close

A clear presentation tells listeners its purpose, previews the main points, develops them in an easy order and ends with a summary or next step. Signposting phrases help the audience follow transitions. Slides should support the speaker rather than repeat every spoken sentence. Practise timing and prepare for likely questions.

Simple example

“Today I will explain the problem, compare two solutions and recommend one” previews a structure.

Exam takeawayDescribe audience, purpose, signposts and a suitable conclusion.
14

Active listening and turn-taking

Listening is an active process: attend to the speaker, identify the main message, notice supporting details and check understanding. Turn-taking lets all participants contribute without interruption. A concise paraphrase can confirm meaning before a response, especially when a point is unclear or sensitive.

Simple example

So your main concern is the deadline, and you need the file by Thursday—have I understood correctly?

Exam takeawayShow listening through an accurate summary and a relevant follow-up question.
15

Clear pronunciation and intelligibility

Pronunciation work supports understanding rather than imitation of one accent. Stress, rhythm, vowel and consonant contrasts can change meaning. Slow down at key words, group information into phrases and articulate endings that carry grammar such as plural -s or past -ed.

Simple example

Record 'worked' and 'work' in a sentence; the final sound changes the time information.

Exam takeawayPractice the sound contrast and use it in a meaningful sentence.
16

Presentation structure and signposting

A short presentation needs a clear opening, a small number of organized points, transitions and a conclusion that restates the message. Signposting phrases tell listeners where they are in the talk. Slides should support the spoken explanation with readable keywords, diagrams or data rather than duplicate a script.

Simple example

Today I will explain the problem, compare two solutions, and recommend one based on cost and access.

Exam takeawayState the purpose early and make the order of points easy to follow.
17

Speaking with useful examples

Examples make abstract explanations concrete and give listeners something they can picture. Select an example that directly matches the concept, explain the connection, and avoid adding so much detail that it overwhelms the main point. A small analogy can help, but mention where the analogy stops matching reality.

Simple example

Explain a queue using people waiting at a counter, then connect the analogy to first-in, first-out processing.

Exam takeawayName the concept, show the example and explicitly state the link.
18

Managing speaking anxiety

Preparation reduces avoidable uncertainty. Rehearse the opening and transitions, use a small cue card rather than memorizing every sentence, and practice in the room or format where possible. Slow breathing and a brief pause help. The goal is understandable communication, not a flawless performance.

Simple example

Prepare three cue words—problem, evidence, recommendation—instead of writing a full speech on a card.

Exam takeawayUse a simple outline, practice aloud and pause rather than rushing after a mistake.
19

Questioning and responding

A useful question is specific enough to invite a focused response. When answering, address the question first, then add a reason or example. If you do not know, say what you can support and explain how you would check the missing detail. Respectful disagreement focuses on an idea and evidence.

Simple example

Could you show which test case produced that result? is more actionable than 'Why is it wrong?'.

Exam takeawayAnswer the asked point directly and support it with one relevant reason or example.

How the unit fits together

Connect the definitions, methods and applications before you start memorizing.

Oral communication combines word choice, sound and interaction. Pronunciation helps listeners identify words; stress highlights a syllable or an important contrast; rhythm shapes the pattern of strong and weak syllables; intonation moves pitch across an utterance. These signals help a listener interpret whether information is new, contrasted, questioned or complete. Listening is equally active: give attention, let the other person finish and ask a follow-up that connects to what they said. A presentation differs from casual conversation because the speaker plans a route through limited time. Signposting words such as first, next and finally make the structure audible.

Quick recap: teach these links back

01

Voice features carry meaning

Pronunciation is how a sound or word is produced. Stress makes a syllable or word prominent. Rhythm is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in speech. Intonation is the rise and fall of pitch; it can signal a question, emphasis, contrast or attitude. Speaking clearly does not mean speaking loudly.

02

Listening is active communication

Let the other person finish, show that you are following, and ask a relevant question when something is unclear. Avoid interrupting or preparing your reply so intensely that you miss the point. In a workplace, choose respectful, concise language that suits the listener and situation.

03

Presentations guide an audience

A formal presentation has a purpose and planned route. Open by naming the topic and why it matters, preview a few sections, explain each point with an example, and close with a summary or next step. A casual conversation can change direction more freely, but both forms need clarity and attention to the listener.

EXAMPLE 1

Use stress to show a contrast

TaskSay “I wanted the BLUE notebook” after someone offers a red one.

  1. Keep the sentence words clear and at a comfortable pace.
  2. Give BLUE more prominence with a little extra pitch or duration.
  3. Do not shout; emphasis comes from contrast, not volume alone.
  4. Listen to whether the audience can identify which feature matters.
Answer and reason

Word stress directs attention to the contrast in color.

EXAMPLE 2

Plan a two-minute presentation

TaskPresent one useful study habit to classmates.

  1. Opening: name the habit and why it matters.
  2. Signpost: “First, choose one small topic; next, recall it without looking.”
  3. Give an example: answer three questions, then check what was missed.
  4. Close with the main takeaway and invite one relevant question.
Answer and reason

A clear purpose, signposts, example and closing help listeners follow.

QUESTION 1Explain stress, rhythm and intonation.+

Stress gives prominence to a syllable or word. Rhythm is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in connected speech. Intonation is the pitch movement across a phrase or sentence. Together they help listeners identify focus, contrast and whether a speaker is asking, concluding or expressing uncertainty. Effective communication uses these features naturally and clearly, not by speaking loudly.

QUESTION 2How does a formal presentation differ from a casual conversation?+

A formal presentation has a planned purpose and structure, uses language suited to the audience, and usually has limited time. It opens by stating the topic, develops a small number of organized points with examples, and ends with a summary or action. A casual conversation is more interactive and can shift topics spontaneously. Both require clear speech and attention to the listener.

WORK IT THROUGH

Plan a two-minute presentation about a study habit.

  1. Opening: name the habit and why it helps.
  2. Middle: explain two steps and give a practical example for each.
  3. Closing: summarize the idea and invite a question. Speak steadily, pause at section changes and look at the audience.

WATCH OUT FOR

Easy mix-ups

  • Stress means prominence, not simply loudness.
  • Intonation is pitch movement; rhythm is the pattern of strong and weak syllables.
  • A presentation needs a clear audience path, not disconnected facts.
