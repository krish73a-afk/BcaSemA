01

Sustainable development

Sustainable development balances environmental protection, social wellbeing and economic activity. A proposal should be judged by more than output or profit: consider health, livelihoods, fairness, resource use and long-term effects. The balance does not mean every impact disappears; it means decisions identify impacts, compare alternatives and reduce harm while meeting real needs.

Simple example

A factory plan should consider jobs alongside water use, emissions, worker safety and local health.

Exam takeawayName the environmental, social and economic dimensions and apply them to a case.
02

Business responsibility and sustainable practice

Businesses can measure energy, water, materials, waste and emissions, then improve efficiency and prevent pollution at its source. They can reduce packaging, design durable products, reuse or recycle materials, choose suppliers responsibly and protect workers and nearby communities. Clear reporting makes progress checkable. Sustainability works best when it affects design and daily operations rather than being only a publicity message.

Simple example

A company can track water used per product, repair leaks, reuse process water where safe and report the reduction.

Exam takeawayGive practical actions across measurement, prevention, design and accountability.
03

Environmental laws named in the guide

The unit names three Indian laws and their broad aims: the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 addresses water pollution; the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 addresses air pollution; and the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 provides a broader framework for protecting and improving the environment. These are introductory purposes; detailed rules and amendments should be checked in current official text.

Simple example

For an exam overview, pair each Act's name and year with its broad environmental purpose.

Exam takeawayLearn the full name, year and purpose; avoid claiming every compliance detail from memory.
04

Environmental assessment and affected communities

Environmental assessment identifies likely effects of a project before major decisions are finalized. It can examine land, water, air, biodiversity, health, livelihoods and social impacts; compare alternatives; and propose avoidance, reduction, rehabilitation and monitoring. Consultation helps include people who may be displaced or affected. Resettlement, rehabilitation and compensation need fair planning, not just a brief mention after construction begins.

Simple example

For a road project, map homes and habitats, compare route options, consult residents and monitor noise and water impacts.

Exam takeawayOrganize the answer as project → impacts → alternatives → safeguards → consultation and follow-up.
05

Climate action missions in India

The guide lists eight National Action Plan on Climate Change missions: solar energy, enhanced energy efficiency, sustainable habitat, water, sustaining the Himalayan ecosystem, Green India, sustainable agriculture and strategic knowledge for climate change. Learn the scope of each in the wording used by your course. Together they connect energy, cities, water, ecosystems, farming and research.

Simple example

A city energy-efficiency program connects climate planning with buildings and everyday electricity use.

Exam takeawayRecall all eight mission themes and explain one with a concrete example.
06

The three pillars and Sustainable Development Goals

Sustainable development considers environmental protection, social wellbeing and economic activity together. The Sustainable Development Goals provide a shared set of goals on areas such as health, education, water, energy and responsible consumption. A project may support one goal while harming another, so good planning identifies trade-offs and checks who benefits.

Simple example

A solar pump can expand irrigation, but a water-use plan is needed so cheaper pumping does not exhaust an aquifer.

Exam takeawayName the connected dimensions and discuss at least one trade-off or safeguard.
07

Corporate responsibility and resource accounting

Businesses affect the environment through materials, energy, water, emissions, waste and supply chains. Measuring these inputs and outputs makes it possible to set targets, find losses and report progress. Responsibility also includes safe working conditions, community impacts and honest communication. A claim such as “green” is meaningful only when it can be supported by evidence.

Simple example

A factory tracks water use per product, repairs leaks and reports both the baseline and the reduction achieved.

Exam takeawayGive a measurable action and explain how results would be checked.
08

EIA steps and public participation

A project assessment begins with screening and defining the study scope, then gathers baseline information and predicts likely impacts. It considers alternatives, proposes mitigation, invites affected people to respond and sets a monitoring plan. Participation improves the assessment when communities can understand information, raise concerns and see how responses affected the decision.

Simple example

Residents may identify a seasonal wetland that a short dry-season survey missed.

Exam takeawayInclude consultation as a real decision step, not a formality at the end.
09

India's climate missions as connected actions

The National Action Plan on Climate Change describes missions covering solar energy, energy efficiency, sustainable habitats, water, Himalayan ecosystems, Green India, sustainable agriculture and climate knowledge. They address linked systems rather than a single “climate” activity. For each mission, connect its focus to a practical action and explain the climate or resilience benefit.

Simple example

Efficient buildings lower electricity demand, which can reduce emissions while making indoor spaces more comfortable.

Exam takeawayRecall the mission themes accurately and explain one in a concrete setting.

How the unit fits together

Connect the definitions, methods and applications before you start memorizing.

Sustainable development becomes practical when objectives are translated into decisions that can be measured. A business can track its energy per product, water use, waste, emissions and worker or community outcomes. Environmental assessment uses a similar logic for projects: describe the baseline, predict changes, compare alternatives, choose safeguards and monitor results. Laws provide shared responsibilities and institutional tools, while climate missions organize action across sectors. Learning the law names and years is useful for exams, but a serious project also needs current official rules and local requirements. A complete answer links one policy or business action to a specific environmental or social outcome and explains how that outcome would be checked.

Quick recap: teach these links back

01

Development has three connected parts

Sustainable development balances environmental protection, social wellbeing and economic activity. If a project creates income but damages drinking water or displaces people without support, its benefits and costs are uneven. A sound plan asks who benefits, who bears the impact, what alternatives exist and how outcomes will be checked.

02

Businesses can prevent harm at the source

A business can measure energy, water, materials and emissions; use resources efficiently; prevent pollution before it is created; reduce packaging; make products durable; and recover materials for reuse or recycling. It can also choose suppliers responsibly, protect workers and communities, and report results honestly.

03

Law and climate planning set shared direction

The syllabus names the Water Act (1974), Air Act (1981) and Environment Protection Act (1986): broadly, they address water pollution, air pollution and a wider framework for environmental protection. For precise legal requirements, use current official text. The National Action Plan on Climate Change lists missions on solar energy, efficiency, sustainable habitats, water, Himalayan ecosystems, Green India, sustainable agriculture and climate knowledge.

EXAMPLE 1

Turn a business goal into a measurable plan

TaskA small food company wants to reduce its environmental impact.

  1. Measure water, electricity, packaging and food waste per 100 units of product.
  2. Choose source prevention first: repair leaks, improve process timing and reduce unnecessary packaging.
  3. Reuse safe by-products or recover recyclable material; check health and safety requirements.
  4. Compare results each month and report both progress and remaining impacts.
Answer and reason

Measurement makes improvement visible and keeps sustainability inside daily operations.

EXAMPLE 2

Match each law to its broad topic

TaskA question asks which course law broadly concerns water pollution, air pollution and environmental protection.

  1. Water pollution: identify the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974.
  2. Air pollution: identify the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981.
  3. Broader protection framework: identify the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.
  4. State that these are introductory purposes; precise duties depend on current rules and amendments.
Answer and reason

Pair the Act's full name and year with the correct broad purpose.

QUESTION 1How can a business contribute to sustainable development?+

A business can measure its resource use and emissions, prevent pollution at source, use energy and water efficiently, reduce packaging and recover materials for reuse or recycling. It can choose suppliers responsibly, protect worker and community wellbeing and communicate results honestly. These steps are most meaningful when they are built into purchasing, design and operations rather than treated only as public relations.

QUESTION 2Name the three environmental laws in the syllabus and state their broad purpose.+

The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 addresses prevention and control of water pollution. The Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 addresses prevention, control and abatement of air pollution. The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 provides a broader framework for protecting and improving the environment. These are high-level descriptions; amendments and rules should be checked in the official India Code text.

WORK IT THROUGH

Use a road or dam proposal to build an environmental assessment.

  1. Describe the project and affected environment; identify likely effects on land, water, health, livelihoods and communities.
  2. Compare alternatives, consult affected people and describe measures to avoid, reduce or repair harm.
  3. Include fair rehabilitation where displacement occurs, then monitor whether promised measures work.
