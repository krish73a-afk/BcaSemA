🗣️ GENERAL ENGLISH - I · UNIT I

Vocabulary building

Vocabulary grows when you understand how words are built and how they behave in sentences. This unit covers roots, affixes, synonyms, antonyms and abbreviations, with a study method that makes new words easier to remember.

PICTURE IT

A word is like a little seed: it can grow new meaning when we add parts.

A word can be a little building. The root is the main block; a prefix goes at the front and a suffix can be added at the end to change meaning or job.

  • Separate a root, prefix and suffix in a word.
  • Choose synonyms and antonyms that fit a sentence.

01

Roots, bases and word families

A root or base carries the central meaning of a word. Related words often form a family around that base, which helps you recognize meaning and grammatical role. For example, act, action, active and actively share a related core idea but do different jobs in sentences. A dictionary entry or sentence context helps confirm which form fits.

Simple example

In “The actor acted actively,” act is the base idea; actor names a person and actively describes how an action happens.

Exam takeawayIdentify the base and explain how the related word changes meaning or word class.
02

Prefixes and suffixes

A prefix is attached before a base and often changes meaning: re- in rewrite means again, and un- in unfair reverses or negates a quality. A suffix follows the base and can change meaning or grammatical role: teach becomes teacher; kind becomes kindness. Watch spelling changes such as dropping or changing a final letter, and check the resulting word in context.

Simple example

Help → helpful → unhelpful: -ful means full of; un- reverses the positive quality.

Exam takeawayShow base + affix → new word and state the meaning.
03

Synonyms, antonyms and shades of meaning

Synonyms have similar meanings; antonyms express contrast. Synonyms are rarely identical in every context because tone, formality and common word partners can differ. “Begin a project” and “start a project” are both natural, while a synonym for “large” may not fit every phrase. Learn related words in useful sentences, not only as pairs.

Simple example

“Tiny” and “small” are synonyms; “tiny” sounds stronger and may not fit every formal phrase.

Exam takeawayDefine each relation and use a context-appropriate example sentence.
04

Abbreviations and audience

An abbreviation shortens a word or phrase. Standard forms can save space, but the reader must know them and the task must allow them. Academic answers and formal communication usually need complete, clear wording; informal chat shortcuts may look careless there. Use punctuation and capitalization in the conventional form expected by your course.

Simple example

“Dr.” is widely recognized in a name; an unfamiliar text-message shortcut may confuse an examiner.

Exam takeawayUse abbreviations only when they are standard and appropriate to the audience.
05

A practical vocabulary-building routine

Record each new word with a simple definition, a sentence of your own and a related word or word family. Review by covering the definition and recalling it, then use the word in a new sentence. Context helps: clues around an unfamiliar word may include an example, contrast or explanation. This turns a word list into usable vocabulary.

Simple example

Word: “reluctant.” Meaning: not eager. Sentence: “I was reluctant to guess, so I checked the evidence.” Related: reluctance.

Exam takeawayShow meaning, context and correct use rather than repeating a memorized list.
06

Denotation, connotation and register

Denotation is a word's basic dictionary meaning. Connotation is the feeling or association it carries. Register is the level of formality suited to a situation. Two words can point to a similar idea but create different effects, so choose a word by checking meaning, tone and audience together.

Simple example

“Childlike” often sounds innocent, while “childish” often criticizes immature behavior.

Exam takeawayExplain the difference in meaning and tone using a sentence for each word.
07

Word families and parts of speech

A word family shares a base but may include different parts of speech. A noun names something, a verb expresses an action or state, an adjective describes a noun and an adverb often describes a verb or adjective. Recognizing the required slot in a sentence helps you choose the right form instead of guessing from sound.

Simple example

Decide (verb), decision (noun), decisive (adjective), decisively (adverb).

Exam takeawayIdentify the sentence slot, then choose the word form that fits it.
08

Collocations and natural word partners

A collocation is a common pairing of words, such as make a decision or heavy rain. A phrase may be grammatically possible but sound unnatural if its word partners are unusual. Reading examples and recording a phrase with a new word helps you learn how it is actually used.

Simple example

We say “strong tea” more naturally than “powerful tea.”

Exam takeawayGive a complete natural phrase, not only a one-word definition.
09

Spaced vocabulary recall

Vocabulary sticks when you retrieve it repeatedly over time. Write the word, meaning and your own sentence, then cover the meaning and try to recall it later. Mix old and new words rather than rereading one list passively. If recall fails, check the meaning and try another sentence.

Simple example

Review a new word today, tomorrow and again several days later, each time making a fresh sentence.

Exam takeawayShow the word in a context that proves you know its meaning.

How the unit fits together

Connect the definitions, methods and applications before you start memorizing.

A useful vocabulary strategy studies words as families and as choices in context. First find the base meaning, then see what prefixes and suffixes add. Next check the part of speech, because a noun, verb and adverb fit different sentence positions. Synonyms offer choices, not perfect swaps: register and common word partners affect what sounds natural. A vocabulary record should make you retrieve meaning, not only reread it. Cover the definition, recall it, use the word in a new sentence, and return to it later. Abbreviations are audience choices: the shortest form is not always the clearest form, especially in formal academic writing.

Quick recap: teach these links back

01

Build meaning from word parts

A root carries a core meaning. A prefix comes before it and can change meaning: re-write means write again. A suffix follows the base and may change meaning or part of speech: teach becomes teacher; kind becomes kindness. Some words change spelling when an ending is added, so check the complete word in a sentence.

02

Choose related words by context

Synonyms have similar meanings, but they may not fit every sentence equally well. Begin and start are close, yet natural word choice depends on situation and tone. Antonyms express contrast, such as early and late. Learn them through example sentences instead of memorizing isolated pairs.

03

Use abbreviations and records for the audience

An abbreviation shortens a word or phrase. Standard forms can help in the right setting, while informal shortcuts may be unsuitable in an exam or formal letter. Keep a vocabulary record with the word, a simple meaning, a sentence you understand, and one related word; revisit it and recall the meaning before looking.

PAGE 4 · GUIDED EXAMPLES

Follow every step

Try each task yourself first, then compare your working with the explanation.

EXAMPLE 1

Build a word family

TaskExplain the parts and use of “unhelpfully.”

  1. Find the base: help, meaning to give assistance.
  2. Add -ful to form helpful, an adjective meaning useful or giving help.
  3. Add un- to reverse the adjective: unhelpful means not helpful.
  4. Add -ly to form an adverb: unhelpfully describes how an action is done.
Answer and reason

The word is un- + help + -ful + -ly. Use the final word to describe a verb: “The sign was placed unhelpfully.”

EXAMPLE 2

Choose a synonym that fits the tone

TaskReplace “big” in “The report shows a big increase in costs.”

  1. Identify the meaning: the increase is large in amount.
  2. Choose a precise formal option such as substantial or significant.
  3. Read the full sentence to check natural word pairing.
  4. Keep “big” if the context is informal and no precision is needed.
Answer and reason

“The report shows a substantial increase in costs” is more precise and formal.

QUESTION 1What are synonyms and antonyms? Give examples.+

Synonyms are words with similar meanings, such as begin and start. Antonyms express contrasting meanings, such as early and late. The words are not always interchangeable: begin a project is natural, while start a project is also possible, but usage depends on context. In an exam, provide the definition and use each word in a short sentence.

QUESTION 2Explain how prefixes and suffixes form new words.+

A prefix is added before a base word; for example, re- in rewrite means again. A suffix is added after a base and may change meaning or word class; teach becomes teacher, while kind becomes kindness. Check spelling changes and part of speech. The best answer identifies the base, affix and resulting meaning.

WORK IT THROUGH

Learn the word “careful.”

  1. The base is care. The suffix -ful means “full of,” so careful means taking care or paying attention.
  2. A useful sentence is: “Mira was careful when she measured the medicine.”
  3. A related word is carefully. Notice that -ly makes an adverb describing how an action is done.

WATCH OUT FOR

Easy mix-ups

  • A synonym is similar in meaning, not always interchangeable.
  • A prefix comes before the base; a suffix comes after it.
  • Use abbreviations your audience will recognize and the task allows.
