🌍 ENVIRONMENTAL SCIENCE & SUSTAINABILITY · UNIT I

Environment, resources & sustainability

The environment is a connected system, and people are part of it. This unit introduces Earth's spheres, the resources we depend on, the effects of using them, and meeting today's needs while protecting future choices.

PICTURE IT

We borrow our world from tomorrow’s children, so use it with care today.

Earth is a shared home with rooms that connect: air, water, land and living things. If we change one room, the others can feel it.

  • Name the main Earth spheres and describe a connection between them.
  • Compare renewable and non-renewable resources with care.

01

Earth's environmental spheres

The atmosphere is the layer of gases around Earth; the hydrosphere includes water; the lithosphere is the solid outer land; and the biosphere includes living organisms and the regions where life exists. These spheres interact rather than working alone. Rain moves between air, land and water; plants depend on soil and water and change the atmosphere through photosynthesis.

Simple example

Rain falls from the atmosphere, enters soil in the lithosphere and feeds plants in the biosphere.

Exam takeawayName the spheres, define each and give one interaction.
02

Natural resources

Natural resources are materials and conditions people use, including land, soil, forests, minerals, water and energy. They support food, shelter, health and economic activity. A resource can be renewable or non-renewable, but both can be damaged by careless use. Soil fertility, clean water and healthy forests also depend on ecological processes that take time to recover.

Simple example

A forest supplies timber and habitat, stores carbon, protects soil and helps regulate water.

Exam takeawayClassify resource examples and mention their uses and limits.
03

Renewable and non-renewable resources

A renewable resource can replenish on a human timescale, such as sunlight or a forest that is allowed to regrow. Renewal is conditional: harvesting faster than regeneration depletes the resource. A non-renewable resource forms far more slowly than people consume it, as with many minerals and fossil fuels. Resource planning compares supply, use, impacts and alternatives.

Simple example

A fish stock may renew if catches stay within its ability to reproduce; overfishing can still collapse it.

Exam takeawayDefine each term and explain why renewable does not mean unlimited.
04

How resource use changes ecosystems

Deforestation removes habitat and can increase erosion. Mining changes land and may create waste or pollution. Dams alter river flow, sediment and habitats. Over-extraction of groundwater lowers water tables, while land-use change can fragment ecosystems. Effects depend on scale, location, design and management, so describe both likely benefits and possible costs rather than treating every project as identical.

Simple example

A dam can provide electricity and water storage while changing fish movement and displacing households.

Exam takeawayTrace activity → environmental change → effect on people and ecosystems.
05

Sustainability and environmental equity

Sustainability means meeting present needs while protecting the conditions that let future generations meet theirs. Intergenerational equity asks whether today's choices unfairly reduce future options. Intragenerational equity asks whether benefits, costs and access are shared fairly among people living now. Environmental decisions should consider who uses a resource, who bears pollution and who participates in planning.

Simple example

Groundwater pumping may help current farms but leave less water for nearby households and future users.

Exam takeawayDefine sustainability and distinguish the two forms of equity with an example.
06

Assessing a large dam

A dam may store water, generate electricity and reduce some flood risks. It may also submerge land, change river flow and sediment, affect fish or wetlands, and displace households or tribal communities. Good assessment compares alternatives, consults affected people, plans fair resettlement and rehabilitation, protects ecological flows and checks long-term outcomes.

Simple example

Compare a large dam with smaller storage, watershed repair or demand management before selecting a plan.

Exam takeawayBalance benefits and costs; include consultation, rehabilitation and monitoring.
07

Carrying capacity and resource limits

Carrying capacity is the population or level of use an environment can support over time without losing the resources and processes it depends on. It is not a fixed number: water availability, technology, consumption and ecosystem health can change it. Sustainable planning therefore looks at rates of use and renewal, not only the total amount currently available.

Simple example

A village may use groundwater sustainably when annual pumping stays near recharge, but not when wells are deepened every year to replace a falling water table.

Exam takeawayExplain the limit as a relationship between demand, renewal and environmental condition.
08

Groundwater, recharge and the water table

Groundwater occupies pores and fractures below the ground. Rain or surface water can recharge an aquifer, but recharge may be slow and differs by geology and land cover. The water table marks the upper surface of saturated ground. Pumping faster than recharge can lower it, increase pumping costs or dry nearby wells. Protecting recharge zones and using water efficiently reduce pressure.

Simple example

If a field is paved, less rain may soak into the soil, so nearby groundwater recharge can fall.

Exam takeawayTrace rainfall → infiltration → storage → pumping, and identify where a proposed activity changes the path.
09

Soil, forests and ecosystem services

Soil forms slowly from rock, organic matter, water and living activity. It stores water and nutrients and supports crops, so erosion or contamination can take years to repair. Forests hold soil, provide habitat, store carbon and influence water movement. Resource use should include these less visible services as well as products such as timber or crops.

Simple example

Removing vegetation on a slope lets rain carry topsoil into a stream, reducing farm fertility and increasing siltation.

Exam takeawayName one direct product and one ecological service for the resource in the question.
10

Environmental impact assessment

An environmental impact assessment (EIA) studies likely effects of a proposed project before approval or construction. A careful process describes the project and local baseline, predicts impacts, compares alternatives, plans mitigation and monitors results. Public consultation helps identify effects that technical surveys can miss. An EIA informs a decision; it does not automatically mean a project is harmless or should proceed.

Simple example

For a road, assess habitat fragmentation and noise, compare routes, plan crossings and monitor wildlife after construction.

Exam takeawayOrganize an EIA answer as baseline, impacts, alternatives, mitigation, consultation and monitoring.
11

Land degradation and soil erosion

Land degradation is a decline in the land's ability to support ecological or human uses. Erosion removes fertile topsoil through water or wind; compaction, salinization and contamination can also reduce soil quality. Deforestation, overgrazing, poor irrigation and construction can accelerate damage. Prevention includes maintaining vegetation, contour practices, careful grazing and restoring soil organic matter.

Simple example

Plants on a slope slow runoff; removing them can increase soil carried into a river.

Exam takeawayName the pressure, the soil process and the effect on farming or water.
12

Watersheds and integrated water management

A watershed is the land area that drains to a shared stream, lake or outlet. Activities upstream can affect water quantity and quality downstream, so water planning should follow the whole catchment rather than a single well or village boundary. Rainwater harvesting, recharge protection, efficient use and fair allocation can be combined according to local conditions.

Simple example

Fertilizer washed from fields upstream can increase nutrient levels in a downstream lake.

Exam takeawayMap upstream action → water pathway → downstream user or ecosystem.
13

Forests, mining and competing resource uses

Land and resource projects can create jobs, energy, materials or public services while changing habitats and livelihoods. A balanced assessment identifies who receives the benefit, who carries the impact, how long effects last and whether alternatives exist. Restoration and compensation are important, but they may not recreate every cultural or ecological value lost.

Simple example

A mine can supply minerals while fragmenting habitat and altering local streams.

Exam takeawayCompare benefits and costs across ecological, social and economic dimensions.
14

Resource management and the precautionary approach

Resource management sets limits and practices that keep use within ecological and social constraints. Monitoring can reveal early warning signs such as falling groundwater or declining fish catches. When impacts may be serious or irreversible but evidence is incomplete, a precautionary approach favors careful safeguards and further study instead of assuming no harm.

Simple example

Set a groundwater pumping threshold and reduce withdrawals if monitoring shows the water table falling.

Exam takeawayExplain the monitoring indicator and the action taken when it crosses a limit.
15

Mineral and fossil-fuel resources

Minerals and fossil fuels are generally non-renewable on human timescales because they form over geological periods. Extraction can supply materials and energy while disturbing land, using water and producing waste. Resource efficiency, recycling and alternatives can reduce demand, but they do not make extraction impact-free. Compare availability, use and environmental cost.

Simple example

Recycling aluminium can reduce demand for new ore and the energy needed for primary production.

Exam takeawayClassify the resource and trace extraction through use to waste or recovery.
16

Renewable energy and ecological trade-offs

Solar, wind and hydropower use replenishing energy flows, but their infrastructure still needs land, materials and planning. A dam can alter river ecology; a solar installation can compete for land; wind projects need wildlife and community assessment. Compare life-cycle impacts and local conditions rather than treating any technology as impact-free.

Simple example

A rooftop solar system may avoid new land conversion while reducing grid electricity demand.

Exam takeawayName the energy benefit and one local trade-off or safeguard.
17

Overgrazing and desertification

Overgrazing occurs when livestock remove vegetation faster than it can recover. Bare soil becomes more vulnerable to erosion, compaction and water loss. In dry regions, repeated degradation can contribute to desertification, a broader decline in land productivity. Rotational grazing, stocking limits and restoration need to match local ecology and livelihoods.

Simple example

Allowing a degraded pasture to rest can help vegetation recover and protect topsoil.

Exam takeawaySeparate the pressure, soil change and long-term land outcome.
18

Groundwater overdraft and subsidence

When groundwater extraction exceeds recharge over time, aquifer levels fall and wells may become deeper or fail. In some regions, removing water from compressible sediments can cause land subsidence, which may be irreversible and increase flood risk. Monitoring water levels and regulating pumping are more effective than responding only after wells dry.

Simple example

A falling water table plus sinking land indicates both supply stress and possible ground damage.

Exam takeawayExplain the link from pumping to water-level decline and any secondary effect.
19

Environmental justice in resource decisions

Environmental justice asks whether people share environmental benefits and burdens fairly and whether affected groups can participate in decisions. A project may provide electricity broadly while concentrating displacement or pollution locally. Analyze distribution, recognition of community rights and access to decision-making, not only total economic benefit.

Simple example

Compare who receives irrigation water with which households lose land for a canal.

Exam takeawayIdentify winners, affected groups, voice in decisions and remedy.
20

Carrying capacity is dynamic

Carrying capacity depends on resource availability, ecosystem condition, consumption and technology. A population or activity may exceed local capacity temporarily by drawing down stored resources, but this can reduce future support. Capacity changes with drought, restoration and infrastructure, so it should be monitored rather than treated as a permanent fixed number.

Simple example

A reservoir can support high water use for one dry season while long-term withdrawals remain unsustainable.

Exam takeawayDistinguish short-term supply from a sustainable long-term rate.

How the unit fits together

Connect the definitions, methods and applications before you start memorizing.

Environmental decisions are easiest to understand when you trace connections. A resource is part of a system: water is stored in rivers, soil, groundwater and ice; forests affect soil, water and habitat; energy choices affect air quality and climate. An action can create benefits and costs at the same time. Sustainability asks whether people can keep receiving important benefits without exhausting the systems that provide them. Equity adds the human question: who gets the benefit, who carries the harm, who gets a voice and what options remain for future generations? For a project such as a dam, use a whole-system assessment rather than saying only “good” or “bad.” Compare alternatives and explain how design and operation change impacts.

Quick recap: teach these links back

01

Earth's spheres work together

The atmosphere is air; the hydrosphere is water; the lithosphere is solid outer land; and the biosphere is living organisms and their habitats. These are not sealed boxes. Rain moves water through air and land, soil supports plants, and organisms change air and soil. Human settlements, farming and industry interact with all four.

02

Resources have limits and impacts

Natural resources include soil, forests, water, minerals, energy and land. Renewable resources can replenish on a human timescale, but only if use stays within their regeneration rate; a forest can still be depleted by overharvesting. Non-renewable resources form much more slowly than people use them. Dams, mining, deforestation, erosion and land-use change can bring benefits and costs.

03

Sustainability joins needs with fairness

Sustainable choices meet present needs while keeping future generations able to meet theirs. Intergenerational equity considers people in the future. Intragenerational equity asks whether people living today share benefits, costs and access fairly. A groundwater decision, for example, affects today's households and the supply left for future users.

Follow every step

Try each task yourself first, then compare your working with the explanation.

EXAMPLE 1

Assess a groundwater decision

TaskA town pumps more groundwater for farms and homes. Explain the sustainability and equity questions.

  1. Identify the resource and its recharge rate; pumping faster than recharge can lower the water table.
  2. List present benefits: water for crops, households or jobs. Then identify who receives them.
  3. Check present costs: nearby wells may fail or water quality may change; poorer households may have fewer alternatives.
  4. Ask what remains for future residents and compare conservation, recharge and allocation options.
Answer and reason

Discuss both intergenerational and intragenerational equity, not only total water supply.

EXAMPLE 2

Build a balanced dam assessment

TaskGive a clear plan for assessing a proposed dam.

  1. Describe intended benefits such as storage, electricity or flood management.
  2. Map impacts on river flow, sediment, habitats, homes and livelihoods.
  3. Compare alternative locations or demand-management options and consult affected communities.
  4. Plan ecological flows, rehabilitation, compensation and long-term monitoring.
Answer and reason

A strong answer balances benefits and costs with alternatives, participation and follow-up.
