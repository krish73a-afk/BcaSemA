Counting & recurrences

Counting turns “how many?” into a choice of rule. Recurrences define a sequence using earlier terms. This unit helps you decide whether order matters, count arrangements correctly and solve simple recurrence patterns.

PICTURE IT

If you have 3 shirts and 2 hats, you can make 3 × 2 outfits. Counting is organized choosing.

If you choose a shirt and then a hat, each shirt can pair with each hat. Counting is careful bookkeeping so no choice is missed or counted twice.

  • Choose permutations or combinations based on whether order matters.
  • Apply addition, multiplication and pigeonhole principles.
  • Solve a simple constant-coefficient recurrence using its characteristic equation.

01

Addition and multiplication principles

The addition principle counts mutually exclusive alternatives by adding their possibilities. The multiplication principle counts a sequence of choices by multiplying the number of options at each stage. First decide whether a task is “this choice or that one” or “this choice and then another.” Avoid multiplying overlapping alternatives or adding stages that happen together.

Simple example

Choose one of 3 shirts and one of 2 hats: 3×2=6 outfits. Choose a shirt or a hat as one gift: 3+2=5 options.

Exam takeawayDescribe the choice structure before writing the calculation.
02

Permutations: order matters

A permutation counts ordered arrangements. For n distinct items selected r at a time without repetition, nPr=n!/(n−r)!. Each position has fewer options after an item is used. Use permutations for roles, rankings or codes where changing order creates a different outcome. Adjust the method if repetition is allowed or some items are identical.

Simple example

Four ordered letters from eight distinct letters: 8P4=8×7×6×5=1,680.

Exam takeawayExplain why different orders count separately, then show the formula and substitution.
03

Combinations: order does not matter

A combination counts selections where only the chosen group matters. For n distinct items chosen r at a time, nCr=n!/[r!(n−r)!]. The r! division removes the repeated orderings of the same group. Combinations are useful for selecting committees, teams or subsets without assigning positions.

Simple example

Choosing 2 students from 4 gives 4C2=6 groups; swapping the same two students does not create another group.

Exam takeawayState that order is irrelevant and show how repeated arrangements are removed.
04

Pigeonhole principle

If more objects are placed into fewer boxes, at least one box must contain two or more objects. The basic form needs no probability: it is guaranteed by counting. A stronger form says that placing N objects into k boxes makes some box hold at least ceiling(N/k) objects. Identify the objects and boxes in an application.

Simple example

Among 13 people and 12 birth months, at least two people share a birth month.

Exam takeawayName the objects and categories, then compare their counts.
05

Binomial theorem

The binomial theorem expands (x+y)^n as the sum of C(n,r)x^(n−r)y^r from r=0 to n. The coefficients are combinations because each term counts the ways to choose which r of the n factors contribute y. Powers of x decrease as powers of y increase, and each term's exponents add to n.

Simple example

(x+y)^3 = x³ + 3x²y + 3xy² + y³.

Exam takeawayWrite terms in descending x power and check every term has total degree n.
06

Recurrence relations and characteristic roots

A recurrence defines each sequence term using earlier terms; initial values identify the particular sequence. For a linear recurrence with constant coefficients, try a solution aₙ=rⁿ and substitute it to form a characteristic equation. Solve for roots, write the general solution, then use the initial values to solve for constants. Repeated roots require a modified form.

Simple example

aₙ=3aₙ₋₁−2aₙ₋₂ gives r²−3r+2=0, with roots 1 and 2.

Exam takeawayShow the characteristic equation, roots and use of every initial condition.
07

Addition and multiplication rules of counting

Use the addition rule when choosing one of disjoint alternatives: if a task can be completed in m ways by route A or n ways by route B, there are m+n choices. Use the multiplication rule for stages that all occur: m choices followed by n choices give mn outcomes. Decide whether the choices are alternatives or successive steps before calculating.

Simple example

Choose one of 3 buses or 2 trains: 5 options. Choose a bus and one of 2 seats: 3×2 combinations.

Exam takeawayExplain why addition or multiplication fits the structure of the choice.
08

Permutations with repeated objects

The formula nPr counts ordered selections of r distinct objects from n. If some objects repeat, dividing by the factorial of each repeated count removes duplicate arrangements. Always decide whether objects are distinct and whether repeated items should be treated as identical; the answer depends on that interpretation.

Simple example

The distinct arrangements of AAB are 3!/2!=3: AAB, ABA, BAA.

Exam takeawayState whether order matters and account for repeated objects.
09

Binomial coefficients and Pascal's triangle

The coefficient nCr counts how many ways to choose r items from n and also appears in the expansion of (x+y)ⁿ. Pascal's triangle builds each interior value by adding the two above it, which follows from splitting choices into those that include one fixed item and those that do not. This gives a fast check on expansion coefficients.

Simple example

The row for n=3 is 1,3,3,1, so (x+y)³=x³+3x²y+3xy²+y³.

Exam takeawayMatch the power of x and y to the chosen term and verify coefficients sum to 2ⁿ.
10

Characteristic equations for recurrences

For a linear recurrence with constant coefficients, try a solution of the form rⁿ. Substitution produces a characteristic equation. Distinct roots give a sum of root powers; repeated roots require an additional factor such as n. Initial values determine the unknown constants. Check the result by computing the first terms of the original recurrence.

Simple example

For aₙ=3aₙ₋₁−2aₙ₋₂, r²−3r+2=0 has roots 1 and 2, so aₙ=C₁+C₂·2ⁿ.

Exam takeawayShow the characteristic polynomial, roots, general form and use of initial conditions.

How the unit fits together

Connect the definitions, methods and applications before you start memorizing.

Counting begins by deciding what makes two outcomes different. If order creates a new result, use a permutation; if only membership matters, use a combination. The multiplication principle applies to stages of one choice, while the addition principle applies to separate alternatives. The pigeonhole principle is a guarantee based on counts, not chance. Recurrences describe a different kind of counting or sequence growth: a term depends on earlier terms, and initial conditions select one sequence from many possible solutions. For linear constant-coefficient recurrences, characteristic roots reveal exponential patterns. The binomial theorem uses combinations because each term counts which factors supply y.

Quick recap: teach these links back

01

Count choices with the right principle

Use multiplication for successive independent choices: 3 shirts and 2 hats give 3×2 outfits. Use addition for alternatives that cannot happen together, adding their counts. A permutation counts ordered selections; a combination counts selections where order does not matter.

02

Permutations, combinations and pigeonholes

For n distinct items chosen r at a time, nPr=n!/(n−r)!; nCr=n!/[r!(n−r)!]. The pigeonhole principle says more objects than boxes forces a box to hold at least two. The binomial theorem expands (x+y)^n using combination coefficients; each coefficient counts how many ways to choose that number of y factors.

03

A recurrence builds each term from earlier terms

A recurrence defines terms using previous terms and needs initial values to identify the sequence. For a linear recurrence with constant coefficients, try a term shaped like r^n to form a characteristic equation. Solve for roots, write a general combination of root terms, then use initial values to determine constants.

EXAMPLE 1

Choose between permutation and combination

TaskFrom 8 students, choose a president and vice-president, then choose a two-person committee.

  1. For the two offices, order matters because president/vice-president differs from vice-president/president: 8P2=8×7=56.
  2. For the committee, order does not matter: 8C2=8×7/(2×1)=28.
  3. The same two students can fill offices in two orders but form only one committee.
  4. Always identify the outcome before selecting the formula.
Answer and reason

Office assignments: 56. Committees: 28.

EXAMPLE 2

Solve a second-order recurrence

TaskSolve aₙ=3aₙ₋₁−2aₙ₋₂, a₀=1, a₁=3.

  1. Try aₙ=rⁿ. Then r²=3r−2, so r²−3r+2=0.
  2. Factor: (r−1)(r−2)=0, giving roots 1 and 2.
  3. Write aₙ=A+B·2ⁿ. Use a₀=1: A+B=1; use a₁=3: A+2B=3.
  4. Subtract equations: B=2, so A=−1.
Answer and reason

aₙ=2ⁿ⁺¹−1. Check n=0 gives 1 and n=1 gives 3.

QUESTION 1How many distinct four-letter arrangements can be made from COMPUTER?+

The eight letters in COMPUTER are all different. Since order matters, use a permutation: 8P4 = 8!/(8-4)! = 8 x 7 x 6 x 5 = 1680. If a question allows repeated letters, or includes duplicate letters in the source word, adjust the counting method before calculating.

QUESTION 2Solve a simple recurrence: a(n)=3a(n-1)-2a(n-2), with a(0)=1 and a(1)=3.+

Assume a(n)=r^n. The characteristic equation is r^2-3r+2=0, so (r-1)(r-2)=0 and the roots are 1 and 2. Thus a(n)=A+B(2^n). From a(0)=1, A+B=1; from a(1)=3, A+2B=3. Therefore B=2 and A=-1, giving a(n)=2^(n+1)-1. Check: a(0)=1 and a(1)=3.

WORK IT THROUGH

Count four-letter arrangements from the eight different letters of COMPUTER.

  1. Order matters because ABCD and BACD are different arrangements, so use a permutation.
  2. 8P4 = 8×7×6×5 = 1,680.
  3. If you were only choosing four letters and order did not matter, you would use 8C4 instead.
