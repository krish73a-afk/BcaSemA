Writing practice

This unit combines reading comprehension, precis writing and essays. The common skill is keeping the central meaning: discover what matters, then express it clearly in fewer or better-organized words.

01

Reading comprehension

Comprehension asks you to understand a passage, not just find matching words. Read once for the overall message and again for the main idea, supporting details, tone and structure. Use nearby words to infer an unfamiliar term. When answering, point to evidence in the passage and separate the author's view from your own.

Simple example

If a passage contrasts two plans, identify the comparison before answering which plan the author supports.

Exam takeawayAnswer the exact question and use relevant details from the passage.
02

What a precis does

A precis is a concise version of a passage that keeps its central claim and important supporting ideas. It removes illustrations, repetition and minor details, but preserves the author's meaning and logical order. It should be objective, coherent and written in your own words. Follow any word limit or ratio given in the exam.

Simple example

A long passage about three causes of pollution can become one concise statement preserving all three causes and the author's conclusion.

Exam takeawayDo not add opinions or copy sentences as a substitute for summarizing.
03

Steps for writing a precis

Read for the whole message, then mark the thesis and necessary support. Cross out examples, repeated points and details that do not carry the argument. Draft a shorter version in your own words, keeping the order of ideas. Check accuracy, cohesion, objectivity and length; add a title only when requested.

Simple example

After drafting, compare each sentence with the source and ask whether it preserves a main point or adds something new.

Exam takeawayShow a clear process: understand, select, condense, rewrite and check.
04

Planning a focused essay

Unpack the prompt and decide exactly what it asks: explain, compare, discuss or argue. Write a thesis that answers it. Plan an introduction, two or three body points with examples, and a conclusion that follows from those points. One main idea per paragraph helps the reader see the reasoning.

Simple example

For responsible technology use, plan access to learning, risks of distraction, and a conclusion about purposeful use.

Exam takeawaySpend a few minutes outlining; keep every paragraph tied to the prompt.
05

Timed writing and revision

Under time pressure, reserve time for planning and a final check rather than writing until the last second. Draft complete paragraphs with topic sentences and relevant support. During revision, first check whether the answer addresses the prompt; then check sentence boundaries, agreement, tense, punctuation and word choice. A short, clear answer is stronger than an unfocused one.

Simple example

Use a final minute to verify that the conclusion follows from the body and no paragraph answers a different question.

Exam takeawayBudget time to plan, write and review.
06

Comprehension: main idea and inference

The main idea is the central point a passage develops. Details support it with examples, reasons or evidence. An inference is a conclusion supported by clues in the passage, even when it is not stated word for word. Separate what the text says from what you infer, and point to the clue that supports the inference.

Simple example

If a passage says the sky darkened and people hurried indoors, you can infer rain may be coming, but the passage has not stated that it rained.

Exam takeawayAnswer from the passage and cite the sentence or clue that supports your response.
07

Summary versus précis

A summary briefly restates the main points of a passage. A précis is a compact, coherent account that preserves the central argument and logical order, usually in your own words and within a required length. Neither should add your opinion or copy long phrases. Keep essential ideas and remove repetition and minor examples.

Simple example

A 300-word passage reduced to about one-third length should retain its claim and reasons, not every illustration.

Exam takeawayFollow the length rule given by the question and preserve the writer's meaning.
08

A repeatable précis workflow

Read the passage once for its overall idea and again to mark the main claim and supporting points. Draft a title, remove examples that are not essential, connect the remaining points in the original logical order and count words. Revise for accuracy, grammar and proportion. A short précis still needs a beginning, connected middle and complete ending.

Simple example

If a paragraph repeats a benefit three ways, keep the core benefit and omit the repeated phrasing.

Exam takeawayShow a concise, neutral result that keeps the passage's argument intact.
09

Planning an essay under time pressure

Before writing, translate the prompt into a task, choose a clear position or purpose and make a brief outline. Estimate time for planning, drafting and a final check. Each body point should have a reason and a supporting example. Leave a few minutes to confirm that you answered the actual question and completed the ending.

Simple example

For a 30-minute answer, spend a few minutes outlining, most of the time drafting and the final minutes checking.

Exam takeawayUse the prompt's key terms in your plan so no required part is missed.
10

Literal questions, inference and evidence

A literal question asks for information stated directly. An inferential question asks what follows from clues and relationships. A personal-response question invites a view but still expects support from the passage. Read the command carefully, answer in your own words and use only evidence the text supports.

Simple example

If a character packs an umbrella after checking dark clouds, you may infer rain is expected; do not claim it rained unless stated.

Exam takeawaySeparate direct evidence from inference and point to the clue.
11

Precis tone, person and logical order

A précis should preserve the writer's central argument while removing secondary examples and repetition. Use your own concise wording, retain the original logical order and keep a neutral tone. Do not add personal judgment, new facts or a conclusion the source does not make. A word limit is part of the task, not a suggestion.

Simple example

If the source lists three examples of one reason, keep the reason and omit the extra illustrations.

Exam takeawayCheck meaning, order, neutrality and word count before submission.
12

Essay thesis and evidence plan

A thesis tells the reader the main claim or purpose. An outline turns it into a small number of supporting points, each with evidence and explanation. The plan should answer every part of the prompt and avoid repeating the same reason in several paragraphs. A counterpoint can strengthen an argument when the task permits it.

Simple example

For a claim about campus recycling, plan separate points on facilities, student habits and measurement.

Exam takeawayMake each body paragraph support one distinct part of the thesis.
13

Timed revision and final checks

Under time pressure, reserve a final review rather than writing until the last second. Check that every question part has an answer, paragraph order is logical, sentences are complete and examples are relevant. Correct the errors that most affect meaning first. A short checklist reduces repeated rereading that does not change the work.

Simple example

Use the final two minutes to check omitted subparts, verb tense and the word limit.

Exam takeawayPrioritize content completion, clarity and high-impact errors.
14

Descriptive and narrative paragraph

Description helps readers imagine a person, place or object through selected sensory details and precise nouns. A narrative arranges events in time and shows how a situation changes. Both need a controlling focus: a list of unrelated details does not create a meaningful scene or story.

Simple example

Instead of 'the lab was nice', describe the humming fans, blue status lights and rows of labelled computers.

Exam takeawayChoose details that support one mood, setting or event rather than listing everything.
15

Compare-and-contrast writing

Comparison explains similarities and differences using a consistent basis. Organize by subject (all points about A, then B) or by point (A and B for each criterion). Keep criteria parallel, and explain why each difference matters. A comparison is not complete if it merely names two things.

Simple example

Compare RAM and storage by volatility, speed, purpose and whether data remains after power is removed.

Exam takeawayChoose two or three fair criteria and discuss both subjects under every criterion.
16

Cause-and-effect explanation

Cause-and-effect writing shows how one or more conditions produce an outcome. Distinguish a direct cause from a contributing factor and from correlation. Use sequence markers carefully and explain intermediate steps; otherwise a reader may see two events but not understand the mechanism connecting them.

Simple example

Heavy rainfall saturates soil; the weaker slope may then fail, sending debris downhill.

Exam takeawayDraw the causal chain and explain each link instead of jumping straight to the result.
17

Opinion writing with reasons

A reasoned opinion states a clear position, supports it with relevant reasons and evidence, acknowledges a meaningful counterpoint, then explains why the position still holds or needs qualification. Fairly representing another view strengthens credibility. An unsupported claim or repeated opinion is not a developed argument.

Simple example

Support a campus recycling plan with waste data, explain cost concerns, then propose a phased rollout.

Exam takeawayUse claim → reason → evidence → explanation, and address one counterargument fairly.
18

Process and instruction writing

Instructions should follow the order in which actions must happen. Use specific verbs, one action per step when tasks are complex, and warnings before a risky step. State prerequisites and expected result. A reader should be able to follow the process without guessing what a vague word such as 'it' means.

Simple example

Save the file, choose PDF as the format, select a folder and confirm the exported file opens.

Exam takeawayCheck sequence, action verbs, missing prerequisites and the result of each important step.
19

Proofreading with an editing checklist

A checklist turns proofreading into a repeatable process. Read once for task coverage and organization, again for grammar and sentence structure, and finally for spelling and punctuation. Reading aloud or backward sentence by sentence can reveal omissions. Correct errors that change meaning before polishing minor style choices.

Simple example

Checklist: prompt answered? paragraphs ordered? verbs agree? articles checked? names and figures accurate?

Exam takeawayProofread in passes so that meaning, grammar and mechanics each receive attention.

How the unit fits together

Connect the definitions, methods and applications before you start memorizing.

Comprehension, precis and essay writing all depend on separating central meaning from extra detail. In comprehension, answer from the passage and notice tone, contrast and evidence. In a precis, preserve the author's logic while removing repetition and illustration; a shorter text is not successful if it changes the claim. In an essay, the thesis is a promise to the reader. Each paragraph should deliver one part of that promise using explanation and evidence. Timed writing adds a planning problem: divide the available minutes among reading the prompt, outlining, drafting and checking. A brief plan usually saves time by preventing a long answer that misses the question.

Quick recap: teach these links back

01

Read for the whole message before details

For comprehension, read once to understand the message and again to mark its main idea and support. Notice the author's tone and use nearby words to infer unfamiliar vocabulary. Answer what the passage says, not a related idea you already know.

02

A precis keeps the argument and removes extras

A precis is a shorter version that preserves a passage's central idea, logic and important points. Remove repetition, illustrations and minor details, but do not change the author's claim. Use your own words, keep the order logical, remain objective, and follow the given word limit.

03

An essay needs a focused path

Unpack the prompt and choose one clear position or main claim. Sketch an introduction with a thesis, body paragraphs with one point and an example each, and a conclusion that follows from the argument. Save time to check sentence boundaries and whether every paragraph answers the prompt.

Follow every step

Try each task yourself first, then compare your working with the explanation.

EXAMPLE 1

Condense a paragraph into a precis

TaskSource idea: “Public parks provide places for exercise, help cool neighborhoods with trees, and give residents a shared space. Cities should maintain them even when budgets are tight.”

  1. Find the central claim: parks provide important public benefits and should be maintained.
  2. Keep the three supporting benefits: exercise, cooling and shared space.
  3. Remove repeated phrasing and write in your own words.
  4. Check that the source's position remains unchanged.
Answer and reason

“Public parks support health, reduce neighborhood heat and create shared spaces, so cities should continue maintaining them.”

EXAMPLE 2

Plan an essay under time pressure

TaskPrompt: “Should students use digital tools while studying?”

  1. Interpret the task: argue a position, not list every technology.
  2. Thesis: students should use tools selectively when they improve learning and protect focus.
  3. Body 1: explain access or practice benefits with a concrete example.
  4. Body 2: explain distraction or misinformation risks and a safeguard.
  5. Conclusion: restate the balanced position and answer the prompt.
Answer and reason

The outline gives each paragraph a clear job and leaves time to review.
